# Lesson 2: CAC and payback

> **Every figure in this lesson is illustrative and invented for teaching.** Tallow Street Beans is a fictional business. Any resemblance to a real one is coincidence.

Customer acquisition cost (CAC) is what it costs to win one new customer. Payback is how long that customer takes to return it. Tallow Street earns $13 of contribution on each $30 box, one box a month (Lesson 1, illustrative).

## Count all of it, and divide by the right customers

One month of acquisition spend (illustrative): ads $8,000, the part of a marketer's salary spent on acquisition $3,000, design work $1,000. Total **$12,000**. It won 300 new customers: 200 credited to paid channels and 100 who arrived by word of mouth.

| Measure | Sum | Result | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ads only, all customers | 8,000 / 300 | $26.67 | Flattering. Avoid it. |
| Fully loaded, blended | 12,000 / 300 | $40.00 | How the whole business is doing |
| Fully loaded, paid | 12,000 / 200 | $60.00 | Whether the paid channels work |

## The next customer costs more than the average

Raising spend to $18,000 brings 260 paid customers rather than 200 (illustrative). The average CAC is 18,000 / 260 = $69.23, which seems acceptable. The extra 60 customers cost $6,000, so the **marginal CAC is $100**. Decisions about spending more depend on the marginal figure.

## Credit is not cause

Paid channels were credited with 200 customers, but some would have come anyway. In a month with all acquisition spend paused, new customers fall from 300 to 170 (illustrative). The spend really caused 130 customers, so the incremental CAC is 12,000 / 130 = **$92.31**, not $60. A pause or holdout test measures this. Credit reports do not.

## Payback uses contribution, not revenue

At $60 paid CAC, revenue suggests 60 / 30 = 2 months. That is wrong, because revenue also has to pay for the coffee.

Simple payback is 60 / 13 = 4.6, so it is recovered during the **5th box**. Boxes are whole, so round up.

## Churn makes it longer, and can make it never

Some customers leave. At 90% monthly retention (10% churn), each customer won yields less in each later month:

| Box (illustrative) | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Expected contribution from this box | 13.00 | 11.70 | 10.53 | 9.48 | 8.53 | 7.68 |
| Running total | 13.00 | 24.70 | 35.23 | 44.71 | 53.24 | 60.91 |

The $60 comes back in the **6th box**, not the 5th. At the marginal $100 CAC it takes 14 boxes.

The running total has a ceiling: CM / churn. At 70% retention (30% churn) the ceiling is 13 / 0.30 = $43.33. A $60 CAC then **never** pays back, however long you wait. Check the ceiling first.

## Good payback can still run out of cash

You pay CAC now and collect contribution over months, so growth uses cash. Add 200 customers a month at $60, with $13 a month from each. To keep this simple, there is no churn or fixed cost, and the first box ships in the month the customer is won (illustrative).

| Month | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Contribution | 2,600 | 5,200 | 7,800 | 10,400 | 13,000 |
| CAC paid | 12,000 | 12,000 | 12,000 | 12,000 | 12,000 |
| Running cash | -9,400 | -16,200 | -20,400 | -22,000 | -21,000 |

The business needs **$22,000**, not $12,000. Faster growth makes the hole deeper in proportion.

## Prepayment changes cash, not unit economics

Suppose a $300 annual plan for 12 boxes (illustrative), which is $25 a box. Cash arrives at once: $291 after the 3% fee, well above the $60 CAC. But each box now earns 25 - 16.10 - 0.75 = $8.15, so on contribution it takes 60 / 8.15 = 7.4, or the **8th box**, compared with the 5th. The plan helps cash and locks the customer in for the year, but makes each box less profitable. State which kind of payback you mean.

## Remember

1. Count all acquisition cost, and match it to the customers it caused.
2. Spending decisions need marginal CAC.
3. Payback is measured in contribution and adjusted for churn.
4. Check the ceiling, and work out how much cash growth needs.
